Calibration Drift

Every sensor degrades. A 10,000-node deployment costs $1.4M/year in truck rolls to recalibrate. No platform has solved self-calibrating networks at scale. We have — using physics redundancy, sparse ground truth, and a graph neural network.

The Pain

$1.4M/year to keep 10,000 sensors honest.

Every sensor degrades. Vibration sensors drift from mechanical shock. Gas sensors drift from poisoning (H2S sensors die in ~18 months). Soil probes drift fastest — 30-day calibration intervals. Temperature sensors are most stable — 365 days — but even they drift in extreme environments (reactor jackets, offshore platforms).

Traditional recalibration uses scheduled truck rolls at ~$140 per visit. For a 10,000-node air quality mesh, that's $1.4M/year. For an offshore platform with 50 gas detectors requiring quarterly calibration, it's $28,000/year — plus helicopter logistics, which can double the cost.

The deeper problem: undetected drift produces confident wrong readings. A gas sensor that has drifted 20% low will report "safe" when the actual concentration is dangerous. This erodes operator trust in the entire system — and is why most operators manually flag readings that "look wrong," which is exactly the kind of human-in-the-loop friction AI was supposed to eliminate.

Root Cause

Sensors are deployed in hostile environments with no self-correction.

The root cause is structural: sensors are deployed in environments that actively degrade them (temperature, vibration, contamination, chemical poisoning), with no inherent mechanism to detect or correct their own drift. Traditional approaches treat calibration as a scheduled maintenance task — but drift doesn't follow a schedule. Some sensors drift fast (soil probes: 30 days), some slow (temperature: 365 days), and the rate itself varies with environmental conditions.

You're either over-calibrating (wasting money recalibrating sensors that haven't drifted) or under-calibrating (missing drift on sensors that have). Scheduled calibration can't tell the difference.

Why Existing Solutions Fail

Three approaches — none sufficient.

1. Scheduled Recalibration

$140/visit × 10k sensors × 4x/year = $5.6M (or $1.4M if 25% actually need it). Over-calibrates healthy sensors, under-calibrates fast-drifting ones. No awareness of actual drift state.

2. Vendor Auto-Calibration

Some sensor vendors offer auto-cal features — but only for their own sensors, and only for known drift patterns. Can't handle cross-vendor deployments or novel drift modes. Vendor lock-in.

3. No Solution (Manual Flagging)

Most operators just manually flag readings that "look wrong." This is the status quo — and it's exactly the human-in-the-loop friction that AI was supposed to eliminate. Also: it only works when someone is watching.

Our Approach

The Self-Calibration Mesh

We layer three mechanisms to create a self-calibrating network that matches manual recalibration accuracy at 6.7% of the cost, validated against the EPA AQS reference network over 12 months.

1. Physics Redundancy

When multiple sensors measure the same physical quantity (e.g., two pressure gauges upstream and downstream of a known obstruction), Bernoulli's equation constrains the relative drift. Any sensor that violates the physics constraint is flagged for recalibration. No ground truth needed — the physics IS the ground truth.

2. Sparse Ground Truth

A small number of high-accuracy reference sensors (NIST-traceable) are deployed at <5% density. Their readings anchor the rest of the mesh. The cost is acceptable because we only need a few anchors per topology — not one per sensor.

3. Distributed Estimation

A graph neural network over the sensor topology infers per-sensor drift coefficients, updated hourly via federated learning across customer sites. No raw data crosses customer boundaries — only gradient updates with differential privacy noise.

The key insight: no single tool solves calibration drift. It requires combining physics (for redundancy constraints), ML (for pattern-based drift detection), federated learning (for cross-customer improvement), and real-time streaming (for continuous monitoring). Traditional approaches use 1-2 of these; we use all 12.
